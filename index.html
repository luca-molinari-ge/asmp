About the group

We are a small, open reading group based in Asia for anyone — graduate students, faculty, and interested readers alike — working through the metaphysics and epistemology of science. Each season we take up a single book and read it closely, chapter by chapter, rather than surveying a field from a distance.

Sessions are conversational rather than lecture-style. Whoever hosts a session prepares a short summary of the chapter and two or three questions to open discussion; everyone else is expected to have read the chapter, not to have mastered it. Disagreement with the author — and with each other — is the point.

No prior background in philosophy of science is assumed. A willingness to sit with a hard argument for an hour is.

The book

Bas C. van Fraassen — Laws and Symmetry — Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1989

Van Fraassen opens from a simple observation: metaphysicians talk about laws of nature in terms of necessity and universality, while working scientists talk instead about symmetry and invariance. The book asks what follows if we take the scientists at their word.

Across four parts, van Fraassen first argues that none of the going philosophical accounts of law — including David Lewis's — succeed on their own terms, and that the standard arguments for believing laws are real do not go through either. He then asks what is left of scientific rationality once laws are set aside, and answers by turning to inference, models, and symmetry arguments as the actual guides theories give us. The closing part extends the same symmetry-based reasoning to the foundations of probability itself, including the principle of indifference.

The result is a central text of constructive empiricism: an account of science that asks theories to be empirically adequate, not metaphysically true, and that finds in symmetry a substitute for law that does real philosophical work.

Reading plan

We are following the book's own four-part structure. Each part runs across two or three sessions, at a pace the group sets as it goes — usually one chapter, sometimes two, per meeting.

IAre there laws of nature?
What laws of nature would have to be, why David Lewis's account of law does not deliver it, and why necessity, possible worlds, and universals fail to ground it either.
IIBelief as rational but lawless
Whether inference to the best explanation can do the work laws were meant to do, and what a general epistemology looks like once it can't.
IIISymmetry as guide to theory
The semantic approach to theories, and symmetry arguments as the actual tool scientists use in place of law — from classical mechanics onward.
IVSymmetry and the illusion of logical probability
Indifference, symmetry, and probability kinematics — and what happens to the principle of indifference under the same scrutiny.

Format

  • Cadence. Fortnightly, roughly ninety minutes a session.
  • Where. Online, with an in-person meeting when enough of us are in the same city — details shared in the WhatsApp channel.
  • Language. English, with discussion open in whatever language helps a point land.
  • Text. Any edition of Laws and Symmetry (Oxford, 1989) works; page references are given at each session for the standard edition.
  • Cost. None. Bring the book and the willingness to argue about it.
